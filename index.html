CANCER BIOLOGY · RESEARCH

“I keep coming back to one question: how do cancer cells survive what is meant to stop them?”

My training in cancer biology has taken me from cell-based experiments and molecular mechanisms to computational tools and clinical exposure. Across these experiences, I’ve become interested in how cancer cells adapt, how they respond to stress, and what these responses reveal about the biology of disease.

My current research explores questions at the intersection of DNA repair, SUMOylation, and therapeutic response in breast cancer. More broadly, I’m drawn to the challenge of understanding complex biological systems and to the possibility that asking better mechanistic questions can bring us closer to understanding why treatments succeed or fail.

This portfolio brings together the research, skills, and questions that have shaped my journey so far, and the direction I hope to pursue through doctoral research.

To focus exclusively on oncology, I joined the Master of Science program in Cancer Biology at the Nitte University Centre for Science Education and Research (NUCSER), Mangaluru (CGPA: 8.07/10 up to 3rd semester). Observing multidisciplinary tumor boards and limb-salvage surgical decisions reinforced the profound clinical urgency that must anchor every mechanistic question asked at the bench.

M.Sc. Dissertation NUCSER, Nitte DU May 2026 TNBC PARP Inhibitors

Role of Compensatory SUMOylation Mechanisms in NSMCE2-Deficient Breast Cancer Cells

Department of Molecular Genetics and Cancer, NUCSER · Guided by Dr. Vivek Tripathi & Dr. Sudarshan Kini

Read Full Thesis (PDF)
Central Research Question & Rationale
Triple-Negative Breast Cancer (TNBC) lacks estrogen, progesterone, and HER2 receptors, leaving cytotoxic chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors (e.g., Olaparib) as critical therapeutic options. Despite initial responses, TNBC rapidly adapts through replication fork stabilization and DNA repair plasticity. We asked: How does NSMCE2 (the SUMO E3 ligase subunit of the SMC5/6 chromosome maintenance complex) influence cellular survival and migratory capacity under replication stress in TNBC?
Approach & Methods
Compared two TNBC models—mesenchymal MDA-MB-231 and basal-like MDA-MB-468—against luminal ER+ control MCF-7. Conducted total RNA isolation (TRIzol), cDNA synthesis, and RT-qPCR using 18S rRNA and GAPDH reference genes. Overcame RIPA buffer Coomassie interference by establishing detergent-compatible BCA protein estimation protocols for SDS-PAGE and Western blotting (anti-NSMCE2 ~28–29 kDa vs β-actin ~40 kDa). Evaluated cell mobility via wound scratch assays under Olaparib (1000 nM) and assessed ploidy via colchicine metaphase arrest karyotyping.
My Specific Contribution
Independently maintained mammalian cell cultures, optimized cell lysis and protein quantification protocols, executed RNA extraction, qPCR, and Western blot validations across all cell lines. Carried out metaphase spread harvests, performed scratch migration assays, and developed custom Python image processing software to automate objective wound area calculations.
Verified Results & Working Hypotheses
Verified Findings: NSMCE2 mRNA and protein levels were markedly elevated in both TNBC lines compared to MCF-7 (52.7-fold in MDA-MB-468 and 21.6-fold in MDA-MB-231 vs GAPDH). Metaphase spreads demonstrated hyper-triploid chromosome distributions (>60 chromosomes). While Olaparib inhibited migration across lines, MDA-MB-231 exhibited marked migratory resilience (87–89% closure at 24h).
Scientific Distinction: Elevated NSMCE2 is verified; the potential compensatory role of other SUMO E3 ligases (PIAS1/4, UBC9) remains a hypothesis proposed for future siRNA knockdown/rescue investigation.

B.Sc. Dissertation Dayananda Sagar University May 2024 Nanomedicine Molecular Docking

Rutin-Encapsulated PEG-PLGA Nanoparticles for Modulating Autophagy and Metabolism in Cancer

SynTech Bio Solutions Lab, Dayananda Sagar University · Guided by Dr. Pronama Biswas

Read Full Thesis (PDF)
Central Research Question
Rutin is a natural dietary flavonoid with broad anticancer potential, but its clinical utility is hindered by poor water solubility and low bioavailability. We investigated: Can biodegradable PEG-PLGA nanoparticles provide an effective drug delivery carrier, and how effectively does Rutin bind key regulators of autophagy and metabolic reprogramming compared to clinical inhibitors?
Formulation & Synthesis
Prepared nanoparticles via Solvent-Antisolvent Nanoprecipitation. Rutin and PEG-PLGA (55 kDa) were dissolved in organic phase (DMSO/DCM) and introduced drop-wise via syringe pump into an aqueous Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA 30–70 kDa) anti-solvent phase. Evaluated stirring speeds, solvent ratios, and centrifugation across four formulation trials.
My Specific Contribution
Carried out nanoparticle synthesis trials, washing, and vacuum drying protocols. Prepared samples for Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) with gold/chromium sputtering. Performed stereochemical validations (PROCHECK Ramachandran plot, VERIFY3D) and conducted in silico docking simulations with AutoDock Vina, BIOVIA Discovery Studio, and UCSF Chimera.
Verified Results & Findings
Trial 2 successfully yielded uniform, spherical nanoparticles with characteristic surface porosity under FESEM. Molecular docking demonstrated that Rutin functions as an ATP-competitive inhibitor of mTORC1 (-10.6 kcal/mol vs Rapamycin -10.0 kcal/mol) and showed synergistic Multi-Ligand Simultaneous Docking (MLSD) affinities when co-docked with PP242 (-13.558 kcal/mol) and XL388 (-14.389 kcal/mol).

Scientific Approach

Research Perspective: How I Approach Biological Questions

A reflection on mechanistic inquiry, experimental limitations, tool building, and the translational connection between bench and bedside.

01

Looking for Circuitry Behind the Phenotype

Observing that a cancer cell line migrates rapidly or survives a drug concentration is only a starting point. The deeper biological question is understanding the regulatory circuitry that makes that survival possible—whether that is replication fork stabilization, altered post-translational modifications like SUMOylation, or compensatory upregulation of alternative DNA repair enzymes.

02

Confronting Methodological Limitations

Doing science has taught me that results are only as reliable as the methods that produced them. When RIPA buffer components interfered with the Bradford assay, shifting to the BCA assay restored equal loading in Western blots. When manual wound scratch measurements introduced subjective error, writing custom Python software provided consistent, reproducible quantification. Addressing friction in methodology is essential to uncovering genuine biology.

03

Anchoring Molecular Inquiries in Patient Needs

My time collecting clinical survivorship data for sarcoma patients at HCG Cancer Centre grounded my scientific motivation. Observing the complexity of limb-salvage decisions and the long road of patient recovery confirmed that laboratory questions gain clarity and purpose when connected to real clinical vulnerabilities and treatment failure patterns.

04

Clarity from First Principles

Teaching high school chemistry has reinforced the discipline of testing whether I truly understand a concept. Translating reaction mechanisms, kinetics, and chemical equilibrium into clear explanations demands rigorous first-principles thinking—a habit that directly benefits experimental design and collaborative scientific communication.

Doctoral Trajectory

Research Motivation: Intent for Doctoral Study

A general statement of research motivations, scientific questions of interest, and preparation for PhD training in cancer biology.

I am seeking to pursue doctoral research in cancer biology to develop a rigorous, mechanistic understanding of how cancer cells adapt to survive therapeutic stress, and how targeting these adaptive networks can inform more effective translational therapies.

Why Doctoral Training

My progression through undergraduate sciences and postgraduate research at NUCSER has clarified that my long-term commitment is to independent research. A PhD represents the opportunity to dedicate several years to formulating deep, hypothesis-driven questions, mastering advanced experimental methodologies, and contributing foundational knowledge to the field of cancer therapeutics.

Questions That Motivate Me

I am primarily drawn to questions surrounding:

  • Replication Stress & Genome Maintenance: How tumor cells with defective cell-cycle checkpoints protect and restart stalled replication forks to maintain genome integrity under chemotherapeutic challenge.
  • Post-Translational Modification Networks: How dynamic modifications—particularly SUMOylation and ubiquitination—reorganize protein complexes at DNA double-strand breaks and stalled forks to drive drug resistance.
  • Synthetic Lethality & Vulnerabilities: Identifying secondary dependencies and compensatory pathways that arise when specific DNA repair mechanisms are compromised in aggressive subtypes like Triple-Negative Breast Cancer.

What I Hope to Learn & Contribute

During doctoral training, I hope to broaden my methodological repertoire into advanced functional genomics (such as genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 or RNAi screening), high-resolution live-cell microscopy, and patient-derived organoid or translational models. In return, I bring hands-on experience in mammalian cell culture, molecular assays (RT-qPCR, Western blotting, karyotyping), in silico structural modeling, and a proven ability to build computational tools (such as Python image analysis pipelines) to overcome laboratory bottlenecks.
